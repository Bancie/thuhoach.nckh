The Ordered Median Function in Location Theory

Definition and the Role of “Ordered” Aspect

The ordered median function is an objective function in facility location theory that extends and generalizes many classical location objectives by introducing a ranking (ordering) of distances. Given a set of demand points and a candidate facility configuration, we first determine each demand point’s distance (or cost) to its nearest facility. The “ordered” aspect means these distances are sorted (ranked) from smallest to largest, and then weighted according to their rank. Formally, if we have $n$ demand points with distances to their assigned facilities denoted $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n$, let $d_{(1)} \le d_{(2)} \le \cdots \le d_{(n)}$ be the sorted distances in nondecreasing order. The ordered median objective is defined as a weighted sum of these sorted distances:

$$
f(\text{solution}) ;=; \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i, d_{(i)},,
$$

where $\lambda = (\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ is a predetermined vector of weights ￼. Typically one chooses $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$, so that larger distances (those at the high end of the ordering) receive greater or equal weight, reflecting a penalty for serving the farthest demands ￼. In essence, the cost contribution of each demand is based not on an inherent fixed weight, but on its position in the ranked list of distances. This “ordering” of costs is what gives the function its name and unique character. It adds a layer of complexity – the objective now depends on the distribution of distances (which distances are largest, second-largest, etc.), not just the aggregate or individual values.

The role of the ordered weighting is to provide flexibility in modeling different service criteria. By adjusting the weight vector $\lambda$, one can emphasize certain parts of the distance distribution. For example, giving much higher weight to the largest distances (the worst-served customers) makes the objective more “fearful” of long distances – promoting equity or worst-case fairness – whereas equal weights would treat all distance contributions uniformly (an efficiency-focused view). This sorting-based approach essentially embeds a mini ranking problem inside the location problem ￼. The largest distances can be penalized heavily if desired, ensuring that solutions which leave some customers very far away are considered worse, even if the average distance is good. Conversely, one could also down-weight the largest distances if outliers are not a concern, or even emphasize middle-range distances if that were relevant. In summary, the “ordered” aspect means the objective isn’t a simple sum or max of distances, but a carefully weighted sum of the order statistics of those distances, allowing the modeler to blend priorities between average efficiency and fairness to outliers.

Mathematical Description

In mathematical terms, consider a set of facility locations $F$ chosen (for example, $|F| = p$ facilities in a $p$-median context) and a set of clients (demand points) $V$. Let $d(v, F)$ be the distance from client $v \in V$ to the nearest facility in $F$. Collect these distances into a vector $z(F) = (d(v_1, F), d(v_2, F), \dots, d(v_n, F))$ for the $n$ clients. Sort this vector in nondecreasing order to obtain $\bar z(F) = (d_{(1)}, d_{(2)}, \dots, d_{(n)})$ with $d_{(1)} \le \dots \le d_{(n)}$. Given a weight vector $\lambda = (\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$, the ordered median objective value for the facility set $F$ is:

$$
\Psi(F) ;=; \lambda_1,d_{(1)} + \lambda_2,d_{(2)} + \cdots + \lambda_n,d_{(n)},,
$$

i.e. the dot product $\lambda \cdot \bar z(F)$ ￼. Equivalently, one can define $\Delta_F(i)$ as the $i$-th largest distance in $z(F)$ (so $\Delta_F(1)$ is the largest distance, $\Delta_F(2)$ the second-largest, etc.). Then the objective can be written $\Psi(F) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i, \Delta_F(i)$, where typically $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$ so that $\lambda_i$ corresponds to the weight on the $i$-th largest distance ￼. Both formulations are equivalent – they simply index the sorted distances in opposite order – and capture the same idea that each ordered position has an assigned weight.

This ordered median function (OMF) is a single objective that unifies a broad family of location objectives. It was introduced as a modeling tool to “allow modeling nearly all classical location objective functions” in a single framework ￼. By appropriate choice of the weight vector $\lambda$, one can replicate standard objectives or create new intermediate ones. The function is agnostic to which specific customers are at certain ranks – it only cares, for example, “what is the largest distance and how much weight do we give it,” rather than “which particular customer has that distance.” This symmetry and focus on ordered values means the OMF is very general: it inherently considers the shape of the distribution of service distances. It has been extensively studied in location analysis as a powerful and flexible objective function ￼ ￼.

Special Cases and Relationship to Classical Location Objectives

Several well-known facility location objectives are special cases of the ordered median function, obtained by plugging in specific weight vectors ￼ ￼:
	•	p-Median (Minimize Total Distance): This objective minimizes the sum of distances (or equivalently the average distance). It is recovered by using equal weights for all demands. For instance, setting $\lambda_i = 1$ for all $i=1,\dots,n$ makes $\Psi(F) = d_{(1)} + \cdots + d_{(n)}$, which is just the total distance ￼. Thus, the ordered median with uniform weights becomes the standard p-median objective (minimizing total or average travel distance).
	•	p-Center (Minimize Maximum Distance): This objective minimizes the worst-case distance. It is obtained by weighting only the largest distance and ignoring the rest. For example, setting $\lambda_n = 1$ and $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1} = 0$ (i.e. weight 1 on the largest distance $d_{(n)}$ and 0 on all others) yields $\Psi(F) = d_{(n)}$ ￼. In other words, the ordered median with this weight vector collapses to the p-center objective, focusing solely on minimizing the maximum distance.
	•	Centdian (Center-Median Combination): The cent-dian is a compromise between center and median objectives, originally defined as minimizing a convex combination of the total distance and the maximum distance (term coined by Halpern in 1976). This can be represented in the ordered median framework by choosing weights that put heavy emphasis on the largest distance while still giving some uniform weight to all distances. For example, one can use $\lambda_n = \alpha$ and $\lambda_i = 1$ (for all $i$) for some $0<\alpha< n$ (then scale appropriately), which effectively adds a multiple of the maximum distance to the sum ￼. In general, centdian objectives are special cases of OMF (the ordered median function generalizes convex combinations of median and center criteria) ￼ ￼. By tuning the weight vector, one can achieve any trade-off between emphasizing the average distance and capping the worst distance.
	•	Worst-$p$ Distances (p-Centrum or Min-sum of Longest p): Another classic model is to minimize the sum of the worst $p$ distances (sometimes called a $p$-centrum objective). This is useful when we want to ensure not just the single worst case is small, but the worst few cases are collectively small. In the ordered median formulation, this corresponds to assigning weight 1 to the largest $p$ distances and 0 to all others ￼ ￼. For example, with $\lambda_{n-p+1} = \cdots = \lambda_n = 1$ and the rest $\lambda=0$, the objective becomes $d_{(n-p+1)} + \cdots + d_{(n)}$ (the sum of the $p$ largest distances). This p-centrum model is clearly a special case of the OMF. By varying $p$ (from $1$ up to $n$), one transitions smoothly from the pure center ($p=1$ case, minimize max) to the pure median ($p=n$ case, sum all distances).
	•	Trimmed Mean Objectives: The ordered median can even capture objectives like a trimmed mean, where one ignores a certain percentage of the smallest and largest distances to focus on the “middle” range. For instance, a $(k_1,k_2)$-trimmed mean might ignore the $k_1$ smallest and $k_2$ largest distances in the sum. In OMF terms, this is achieved by setting $\lambda_i = 0$ for the lowest $k_1$ ranks and for the highest $k_2$ ranks, and $\lambda_i = 1$ for the remaining middle positions. This is another example of a criterion that OMF can represent ￼.

In fact, most of the fundamental location objectives in the literature are unified under the ordered median framework ￼ ￼. By choosing appropriate weight vectors, analysts can model a continuum of trade-offs between pure efficiency and pure equity objectives ￼. For example, gradually shifting weight from the lower-ranked distances to the higher-ranked distances will move an optimal solution from the p-median solution toward the p-center solution continuously. This flexibility is one of the main strengths of the ordered median approach: rather than formulating a new model for each flavor of objective, one can use the single OMF model and just adjust the $\lambda$ parameters to get the desired balance ￼.

Illustrative Example: Comparing Objectives

Illustration of how different location objectives yield different facility placements on a simple line. In this scenario, three demand points are located at positions 0, 1, and 10 on a line (black dots). We consider placing one facility (red star) to serve them. Left: Using a p-median (min-sum) objective, the optimal facility location is near 1, minimizing total distance (sum of distances = 10). This leaves the farthest demand (at 10) with a distance of 9, as shown by the blue dashed distance lines (lengths 1, 0, and 9). Right: Using a p-center (min-max) objective, the optimal facility is at 5, the midpoint between the two extreme demands, minimizing the maximum distance to 5. This achieves a better worst-case distance (no one is farther than 5), but increases the total distance (sum of distances = 14). An ordered median objective can strike a compromise – for example, by giving extra weight to the largest distance but not ignoring the others, the optimal placement would shift to an intermediate position (e.g. around 3 or 4), reducing the worst-case distance while only slightly increasing total distance. This demonstrates how the ordered median function can balance efficiency and fairness in facility placement.

In the figure above, we see how the choice of objective influences the solution in a simple setting. The p-median solution favors the cluster of two nearby clients (at 0 and 1) – placing the facility at 1 keeps their distances low and thus the total distance minimal, but at the expense of one client being very far. The p-center solution instead equally considers all clients by minimizing the longest distance; it centers the facility between the extreme clients (0 and 10) to ensure no one is more than 5 away, though this means the closer clients travel a bit further on average. The ordered median objective can be tuned between these extremes. For instance, if we assign a moderately higher weight to the largest distance (the far client) and smaller weight to others, the optimal facility might locate somewhere between 1 and 5 (say, around 3). This would reduce that far client’s distance (perhaps from 9 down to, say, 7) at a slight cost of increasing the others’ distances (those at 0 and 1 might now travel 3 and 2 instead of 1 and 0). The trade-off is controlled by the weight vector: heavier weight on the farthest distance pulls the solution toward the center objective, while lighter (more equal) weights push it toward the median solution. This example highlights the essence of the ordered median function: it provides a single framework to achieve different compromises between total travel cost and worst-case (or other percentile-based) service distance.

Applications in Real-World Location Problems

The ordered median function’s ability to balance different criteria makes it highly relevant in various real-world planning contexts:
	•	Logistics and Distribution: Companies locating warehouses or distribution centers often care about average transportation cost (a median-like concern) while also wanting to avoid extremely long shipping routes to any customer (a center-like concern for service level). By using an ordered median objective, a firm can incorporate service level agreements (e.g., “most customers should be within 2 days shipping, and virtually none more than 5 days”) directly into the location model. The weight vector can be tuned so that beyond a certain distance, the penalty increases sharply, discouraging solutions that leave a cluster of customers very far away. This leads to more balanced designs of distribution networks, where neither the overall cost nor the worst-case distance is neglected.
	•	Urban Planning and Public Facilities: City planners locating public services (hospitals, fire stations, schools, etc.) must consider both efficiency (total travel or average response time) and equity (no community should be too far from a facility). A pure p-median model might place facilities only in high-density areas, ignoring sparsely populated outskirts, whereas a pure p-center model might allocate resources inefficiently just to cover a remote area. The ordered median approach allows a planner to find middle ground – for example, heavily penalizing the longest few response times (to ensure reasonable coverage for all neighborhoods) while still accounting for population-weighted total distances. This leads to solutions that generalize the notion of “median” to include fairness: everyone gets a decent level of access, and overall travel remains near-optimal. In practice, this could mean, say, locating a new clinic slightly closer to a remote village than a pure cost-minimizing model would, because the objective “feels” the impact of that village’s distance through a higher weight on that distance.
	•	Service Facility Placement with Quality of Service Targets: In services like ambulance dispatch, fire coverage, or even retail store placement, often there are service level targets such as “90% of the population should be within X distance/time.” Traditional models sometimes handle this via constraints or by solving a series of p-center problems for different radii. The ordered median function offers a more nuanced way: by setting the weight vector so that all but a small fraction of the largest distances are heavily penalized, the solver will naturally try to ensure almost everyone is within a desirable range. Essentially, it can internalize percentile-based targets (like a 90th-percentile travel time) into the objective. This is analogous to minimizing a weighted percentile of distances rather than just the maximum ￼. Such an approach is valuable in emergency services, where you want to keep nearly all response times low, but a strict max threshold might be too rigid or costly.
	•	Flexible and Robust Location Planning: The ordered median framework can model robust or equity-focused criteria beyond the classical ones. For example, one can use weights that correspond to a trimmed mean (ignoring a few outliers on either end) to get a solution that is robust to some extreme values (perhaps useful if a few demand points have highly uncertain or anomalous demand). Likewise, in scenarios involving fairness or social equity, policy-makers might choose weight vectors that greatly increase the penalty for the worst-off percentile of users, effectively directing the solution to improve service for underserved groups. Because the OMF was developed with these “social” or “robust” objectives in mind ￼, it has seen use in public sector planning where decision makers wish to balance overall efficiency with a fair distribution of service.

In summary, the ordered median function serves as a unifying objective in location theory, bridging the gap between sum-based (median) and max-based (center) criteria and encompassing many variations in between. Its “ordered” weighting mechanism gives practitioners a powerful knob to dial the solution toward their preferred trade-off between efficiency and equity. This has practical implications in any domain where facility placement impacts both aggregate cost and individual access: by adjusting the ordered median weights, one can design solutions that closely align with real-world priorities (minimizing total travel, capping the worst-case distance, or anything in between). The concept not only generalizes classical models but also provides a richer language for new models – for example, capturing multi-percentile objectives or robust measures – all within one coherent framework. As a result, the ordered median function has become an important tool in logistics, urban planning, and service location decisions, helping to ensure that facility placement is optimized in a balanced and context-appropriate way. ￼ ￼
